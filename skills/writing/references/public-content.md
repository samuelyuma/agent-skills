# Public Content

Read for UI copy, microcopy, public-service content, help center articles, and any text a non-expert reader must act on. For developer documents, chat, PRs, commits, and code comments, use the core and the use-case files instead.

> Adapted from [bahasa-indonesia-plain](https://gist.github.com/orepras/3e1ca8974fc51d11825f142fa6a66635) and [english-plain](https://gist.github.com/orepras/61f07724a6d02d3f319e3371867dad6a) by [orepras](https://github.com/orepras). See `CREDITS.md` in the source repository for revisions, permission status, and changes.

## Scope and Precedence

- These rules apply to public-facing copy: product strings, public guides, notices, forms, and service content.
- Inside that scope, sentence case and the no-emphasis rule replace the core's Title Case and bold defaults.
- They do not change chat replies, PR descriptions, commit messages, code comments, or developer documents.
- Follow a project style guide first. State the deviation instead of silently mixing conventions.

## Start From the Reader's Need

- Write what the reader must know to do or decide something, not what the organization wants to say.
- Put the most important point first: in the page, each section, each paragraph, and each sentence. Conclusion first, then detail, then background.
- One idea per sentence. One topic per paragraph.
- Be concrete. Name the number, date, person, or action. Avoid "various", "going forward", "in relation to this".
- Cut anything that adds no meaning. Shorter is clearer, but never drop a condition, number, negation, or step.
- Name the actor. "We reviewed your application" beats "Your application has been reviewed."

## Inclusive Language

- Put the person first: "people with disabilities", "a person with diabetes". Some communities prefer identity-first language, such as Deaf or autistic. Follow the community's own choice.
- Avoid labels and stigma: crazy, insane, dumb, lame, slow, weak, crippled, abnormal, "suffering from", "victim of".
- Avoid ableist metaphors: "blind spot", "falling on deaf ears", "turn a blind eye", "tone deaf".
- Do not assume a single norm: "normal family", "normal user", "working-age adult".
- Do not assume gender, marital status, age, or family structure unless the task needs it.
- Do not build humor on ethnicity, gender, religion, disability, or bodies.
- Do not use a person's condition as a metaphor for a system problem.

## Trauma-Informed Communication

- Do not blame. "Please try again in the Email field" beats "You failed to enter a valid email."
- Give control. Say what will happen before it happens. Warn before an action cannot be undone.
- Use calming, factual language when something goes wrong: "Your data is safe." "You can go back at any time."
- Avoid false urgency: "Act now", "Offer ends soon", "You're missing out". If a deadline is real, state the date and time.
- Write errors with three parts: what happened, that the reader is not at fault, and what to do next. Keep error codes exact when the reader needs them to get support.
- For sensitive topics such as mental health, money, legal matters, violence, or grief, acknowledge the weight without dramatizing it. Offer a clear way to stop or leave.
- Do not use surprise consequences, hidden defaults, or shame to drive action.

## Numbers and Dyscalculia

- Do not pile numbers into one sentence. Move three or more figures into a list or table.
- Add a concrete comparison for large amounts: "Rp5 miliar, about the cost of running the office for a year."
- Use sequence words for important steps: "First... Second... Finally..."
- Do not hide calculations. Show the steps or provide a calculator.
- Write dates unambiguously. The format belongs in the language file.
- Give percentages a base: "30% cheaper, from Rp100.000 to Rp70.000", not "30% cheaper".
- Say "noon" and "midnight" instead of 12 a.m. or 12 p.m. when the language allows it.

## Readers Outside the Default

- Do not assume fast internet, a new phone, a bank account, a printer, or a permanent address.
- Do not assume the reader lives in a capital city or knows local brands, holidays, seasons, or sports.
- Write for readers who may use a translation tool: short sentences, literal wording, one meaning per word, few idioms.
- Use examples and names that reflect the audience, not one region or one background.
- For regional services, add a local-language version or note for critical instructions and emergency information.
- State the time zone when readers may be in several.

## Formatting for Public Copy

- Use sentence case for headings, subheadings, buttons, labels, and table headers. Capitalize proper nouns only.
- Do not use all caps for emphasis. Screen readers may spell it out.
- Do not use bold or italic for emphasis. Use structure and word choice. Bold only a literal interface element in an instruction: select **Save**.
- Keep headings under about 65 characters, unique and descriptive. Do not end them with a period, question mark, or exclamation mark.
- Start a bulleted list with a sentence ending in a colon. Begin items lowercase unless they are proper nouns. One idea per item. No semicolons.
- Use numbered steps only when order matters. Start each step with a verb and end with a period.
- Use descriptive link text that makes sense read alone. Never "click here" or "read more".
- Do not use Latin abbreviations: write "for example", "that is", "and so on".
- Do not use "&" outside official brand names.
- Expand an acronym on first use unless the audience already knows it. Avoid "and/or" and "he/she".
- Do not rely on color, position, or shape alone. "The green button on the right" fails for screen reader users and color-blind readers. Name the element.
- Do not use an FAQ to avoid answering the question where the reader needs it. A FAQ is a document type decision, not a place to dump hard content.

## Self-Check

- Can a first-time reader act after one read?
- Is the most important point first?
- Is every sentence active, short, and carrying one idea?
- Are emphasis, jargon, and bureaucratic phrasing removed?
- Are headings and links descriptive and in sentence case?
- Is any word cuttable without losing meaning?
- Is the text safe for someone who is emotionally vulnerable?
- Are numbers and steps presented without overloading the reader?
- Is the language free of stigma and assumptions about a "normal" reader?
- Would a non-native reader or a translation tool handle it?
